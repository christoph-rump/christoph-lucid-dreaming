I’m a certified lucid dreaming facilitator, trainer and coach, with a background in IT consulting.

My mission is to help people integrate their shadows in the context of mental health, from stress to burnout, through mindful day and night routines. Lucid dreaming is the frame that holds it together.

Day and night are one practice.

By day

Small, mindful routines for attention, rest and boundaries, so you notice stress and burnout signals early instead of late.

Awareness is the thread between them.

By night

Lucid dreaming: recognising that you’re dreaming while it happens, and using that awareness for curiosity, calm and self-regulation.

Dreams are also where our shadows show up, the parts of ourselves we usually avoid. With practice, we can meet them and integrate them.

What is lucid dreaming?

Lucid dreaming can sound abstract. It has been studied in sleep laboratories for over 40 years.

A practice with deep roots

Lucid dreaming is practised as a form of Dream Yoga, a contemplative way of working with awareness in sleep. Science followed in the 1970s and 1980s: Keith Hearne and Stephen LaBerge showed in sleep laboratories that dreamers can know they are dreaming and signal it from inside the dream.

What is different from ordinary dreams?

In an ordinary dream we live through scenes and emotions without questioning them. Parts of the prefrontal cortex, at the front of the brain, are less active during REM sleep, when most vivid dreams happen. This is thought to be one reason we accept even the strangest events without a second thought.

In a lucid dream, activity in these regions rises markedly within seconds. Researchers found it in the dorsolateral and frontopolar prefrontal cortex and in the precuneus. These are areas typically active when we are awake and reflect on ourselves, keep track of our own thoughts and make deliberate decisions.

What does that indicate? Lucidity is not a vague feeling but a measurable state in which waking-type self-awareness appears inside a dream. The dreamer is still asleep and still dreaming, yet can reflect, remember an intention and choose how to act.

A 2015 study points the same way: people with high dream lucidity had more gray matter in the frontopolar cortex, a region that is active when we monitor our own thoughts while awake. This suggests a link between lucid dreaming and metacognition, the ability to think about your own thinking.
